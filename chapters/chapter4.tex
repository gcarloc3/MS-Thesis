The Kiessig fringes discussed in~\ref{sec: xrr} are apparent from~\ref{fig: a-Si_XRR} and their size varies depending on the QCM thickness. The largest fringes appear in the \qty{0.035}{\kilo\angstrom} thick film and get progressively smaller as the \gls{QCM} thickness reading increases, with the \qty{1.520}{\kilo\angstrom} film having very small fringes. No fringes could be identified for films beyond this thickness, which is attributed to the limited resolution of the x-ray diffractometer. The step size of the machine is $\Delta\theta = 0.0001$, which implies that any fringes at this scale corresponding to a thickness of approximately \qty{300}{\nano\meter} can no longer be observed. In~\ref{tab: thickness_a-Si} the results of the direct and Fourier method are in general agreement, signifying the accuracy of the \gls{XRR} method in determining the thickness of the film.

Linear regression of the \gls{XRR} and \gls{QCM} thicknesses as shown in~\ref{fig: linear_fit_xrr_aSi} gives
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:aSi_thickness} 
    y = 169.70x
\end{equation}
with $R^2 = 0.9996$, where $y$ is the measured XRR thickness in \unit{\nano\meter} and $x$ is the \gls{QCM} reading in \unit{\kilo\angstrom}. The high $R^2$ value signifies that this equation relating the \gls{QCM} thickness to the \gls{XRR} thickness is reliable and can be used to match the real-time \gls{QCM} readings during deposition to the actual thickness of the deposited film.

\section{Refractive index of a-Si thin films}\label{sec: results_index_Si}

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/swanelpoel_aSi.png}
	\caption[Envelope method for \qty{600}{\nano\meter} a-Si thin film]{The transmission curves for the \qty{600}{\nano\meter} a-Si thin film $T_{a-Si}$ and the glass substrate $T_{sub}$ together with the upper $T_{M}$ and lower envelopes $T_{m}$ for the envelope method. The envelopes cross at $\lambda=$ \qty{630}{\nano\meter}, likely caused by the very few data points used for fitting as highlighted.}\label{fig: swanelpoel_aSi}
\end{figure}

~\ref{fig: swanelpoel_aSi} shows the UV-Vis transmittance spectra of a \qty{600}{\nano\meter} a-Si thin film deposited on a glass substrate together with the upper and lower envelopes as calculated using a modified version of the envelope method described in~\ref{sec: swanepoel}. The equations of the exponential functions fitted to the extrema points listed in~\ref{tab: swanepoel_aSi} using \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| are
\begin{align}
	y &= 0.7848 - 250.7271e^{-x/103.5957} \label{eqn:aSi_env_1} \\
	y &= 0.4543 - 464.5691e^{-x/83.2507} \label{eqn:aSi_env_2}
\end{align}
for the upper and lower envelopes respectively. The last maxima at $\lambda=$ \qty{655}{\nano\meter} is excluded from further calculations due to the mismatch in expected value for $m$. The envelope method in~\ref{sec: swanepoel} specifies that succeeding extrema must correspond to half-integer steps, but the calculated value at this wavelength is $m=5.0$ instead of a value close to the half-integer $5.5$. This likely means that the envelope functions are no longer accurate at this wavelength. The minima at $\lambda=$ \qty{613}{\nano\meter} is also excluded because the calculated $N$ is negative, caused by the crossing of the upper and lower envelopes. More fringes in the transmission spectra would probably result in more accurate envelopes which satisfy the necessary conditions outlined in~\ref{sec: swanepoel}.

The equation for the refractive index of the a-Si thin film according to the Cauchy~\ref{eqn: sellmeier} fitted to the remaining four data points of the refractive indices obtained from the envelope method using \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| is
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:aSi_sellmeier} 
    n(\lambda) = (2.8716\pm0.0347) + \frac{38061.0\pm22671.1}{\lambda^2}
\end{equation}
and the graph of the resulting refractive index versus wavelength is shown in~\ref{fig: index_aSi}. The low $R^2$ is caused by the refractive index at \qty{871}{\nano\meter} deviating from the trend of increasing refractive index for decreasing wavelength, likely due to the selection of the transmission envelope.

On the other hand, direct curve fitting of the Cauchy equation to the transmission spectra using the transfer matrix method and \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| as shown in~\ref{fig: T_fit_aSi} gives the following equation for refractive index 
\begin{equation}\label{eqn:aSi_direct_cauchy} 
    n(\lambda) = (3.2971\pm0.0128) + \frac{0\pm9886.9}{\lambda^2}
\end{equation}
as represented by the blue line in~\ref{fig: index_aSi}. Values lower than \qty{700}{\nano\meter} had to be excluded from the fitting in order for the model to converge properly, which means that the refractive index values corresponding to wavlengths lower than \qty{700}{\nano\meter} are extrapolated.

	\caption[Refractive index of a-Si thin film]{Refractive index of the a-Si thin film from \qtyrange{500}{1100}{\nano\meter} as calculated from the envelope and direct fitting methods compared to values obtained by Swanepoel\cite{swanepoelDeterminationThicknessOptical1983} and Schinke\cite{schinkeUncertaintyAnalysisCoefficient2015}. The envelope method gives a much lower refractive index value compared to the direct method.}\label{fig: index_aSi}

The refractive indices of the a-Si thin film calculated for both methods are quite consistent, differing by around $0.25$, and comparable to the literature values obtained by Swanepoel for a-Si at longer wavelengths\cite{swanepoelDeterminationThicknessOptical1983}. Furthermore, the calculated a-Si thin film refractive index is lower than that of crystalline silicon, which has a refractive index of $3.5$ to $4.25$ across the range of wavelengths as measured by Schinke~\cite{schinkeUncertaintyAnalysisCoefficient2015}. This is likely due to the lower density and less ordered structure of the a-Si thin film compared to crystalline silicon, and supports the previous findings in~\ref{sec: results_thicknesses}. However, the refractive index appears somewhat flat over the wavelength range instead of increasing at shorter wavelengths. This difference from the expected behavior at shorter wavelengths is probably caused by the loss of information due to the fitting procedures used.

In the modified envelope method, the number of data points used to fit the transmission envelopes is too few, which results in less accurate transmission envelopes and consequently refractive index values. On the other hand, for the direct fitting method using the transfer matrix method, the accuracy of the value depends on the quality of the fit to the experimental data and is also limited to the \qtyrange{700}{1100}{\nano\meter} wavelength range, consequently extrapolation to shorter wavelengths may no longer be accurate. 

\section{Refractive index of SiO\texorpdfstring{$_{2}$}{2} thin films}\label{sec: results_index_SiO2}
~\ref{fig: intensity_SiO2} shows the reflectance spectra of a \qty{990}{\nano\meter} SiO$_2$ thin film deposited on a Si substrate after deconvolution using a mirror. For the extrema method there are six points that can be used for the calculation of the refractive index of the film using~\ref{eqn: index_extrema}; they are listed in~\ref{tab: oscillation_SiO2} together with the corresponding refractive indices and the new average wavelengths. Fitting the resulting data points to the Cauchy~\ref{eqn: sellmeier} using \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| as shown in the green points and blue curve in~\ref{fig: index_SiO2} gives the following equation:
\begin{equation} \label{eqn:SiO2_extrema_cauchy} 
    n(\lambda) = (1.479980\pm0.028405) + \frac{3669.94\pm13332.23}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}

On the other hand, direct fitting using \verb|scipy.optimize.curve_fit| and the transfer matrix method to fit the Cauchy equation directly to the reflectance data results in the following equation for the refractive index as shown in the red curve in~\ref{fig: index_SiO2}:
\begin{equation} \label{eqn:SiO2_direct_cauchy} 
    n(\lambda) = (1.431606\pm0.012136) + \frac{9540.05\pm4793.93}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}

\end{figure}

	\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figures/index_SiO2.png}

The high uncertainty for the extrema method may be caused by the few data points fitted.}\label{fig: index_SiO2}
\end{figure}

The refractive indices of the SiO$_2$ thin film obtained from both methods are comparable to the literature values obtained from Gao\cite{liuOptimalGrowthConditions2022}. The extrema method has a slightly higher value versus the literature and a much uncertainty, whereas the direct method has a slightly lower value and smaller uncertainty. The increased uncertainty in refractive index values for the extrema method compared to the direct method is likely caused by the few data points used for fitting in the extrema method compared to the direct method. 

The slightly lower refractive index value calculated through the direct method may be caused by multiple factors, such as increased porosity of the film or poor fitting through the transfer matrix method. The refractive index of the film appears to be close to that of stoichiometric SiO$_2$ even though there was no reactive gas used during the deposition process. Miyazaki et al.\ deposited SiO$_{1.74}$ thin films and found that the refractive index at \qty{633}{\nano\meter} was $1.50$~\cite{miyazakiRefractiveIndexDielectric2010}, which is higher than the measured refractive index for both methods. Increased porosity may also account for the lower density of the SiO$_2$ thin film as measured previously by XRR compared to bulk SiO$_2$.

\section{Transfer matrix method for antireflective coatings}
The simplest design for an antireflective coating as stated in~\ref{sec: EM waves} is a quarter-wavelength thick film with refractive index equivalent to the square root of the substrate. The problem with this antireflective coating is that it only works for the chosen wavelength and severely limits the choice of materials for the film. Since the goal is to create an antireflective coating which works at multiple wavelengths while utilizing readily available materials, the single-layer coating is ruled out.

Moving to the two-layer antireflective coating with a-Si first on the substrate and SiO$_2$ on top, the results of the transfer matrix simulation are presented in~\ref{fig: two_layer}. Using the literature values of the refractive indices for a-Si and SiO$_2$, the simulation started from a $51/51$ \unit{\nano\meter} a-Si/SiO$_2$ stack and ended at a $73/153$ \unit{\nano\meter} stack. The reflectance is lower compared to approximately \qty{30}{\percent} across the wavelength range for bare Si, but a much better design can probably be obtained.

\end{figure}

The three-layer antireflective coating of SiO$_2$/a-Si/SiO$_2$ as seen in~\ref{fig: three_layer} is much more suitable. After four iterations from $51/51/51$ \unit{\nano\meter}, the optimized thickness for the multilayer stack is $48/19/208$ \unit{\nano\meter} for the bottom, middle, and top films, with a much lower reflectance across the wavelength range compared to the two-layer stack previously presented. The reflectance at both the \qty{780}{\nano\meter} and \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} wavelengths is less than \qty{1}{\percent}.

The comparison of the different methods in obtaining the refractive index for the films show that using the direct method of fitting the Cauchy equation to the transmittance spectra for a-Si results in the most accurate simulations of the minimum wavelength for the multilayer antireflective coating. The envelope method generally underestimates the reflectance of the coatings. For SiO$_2$, the difference between the direct and extrema method is marginal since the difference in refractive indices for the two methods is only around $0.05$, compared to the much larger gap of approximately $0.25$ for the direct and envelope method used for a-Si.

All of the experimental spectra for the multilayer antireflective coatings have reduced values of reflectance compared to the bare wafer, proving their effectiveness as antireflective coatings. The offset in the minimum reflectance values obtained experimentally versus the simulation is likely caused by the nature of the experimental setup, which measures the specular reflectance of the sample relative to a mirror which serves as baseline. More accurate reflectance values would probably be obtained from absolute measurements which consider the total amount of light incident and reflected from the sample, but this setup requires an integrating sphere in order to collect all the reflected light and is much more complex.
