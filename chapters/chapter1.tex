\chapter{Introduction}\label{cha: introduction}
The development of devices which operate in the terahertz range has been a recent area of interest for researchers. The applications of terahertz waves include analysis of biological specimens~\cite{yuMedicalApplicationTerahertz2019}, pharmaceuticals~\cite{dohiApplicationTerahertzPulse2016}, and imaging~\cite{petrovApplicationTerahertzPulse2016} among others. The mechanisms by which these devices operate may include upconversion from the radio and mmWave range, or downconversion of photonic devices from the infrared region~\cite{songReviewIntenseTerahertz2026}. An example of a device which utilizes downconversion is the terahertz \gls{PCA}, and there are already multiple demonstrations and even commercial \gls{PCA} devices that utilize the \qty{780}{\nano\meter} and \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} wavelengths, which are supplied by Ti:sapphire and telecommunication fiber lasers respectively~\cite{yangSimpleReliableOptical2019, PCAPhotoconductiveAntenna}. A \gls{PCA} consists of a semiconductor material where the terahertz generation or absorption occurs and two metal electrodes on the semiconductor surface separated by a few microns that forms the photoconductive gap~\cite{songHandbookTerahertzTechnologies2015}. When a femtosecond pulse from the laser illuminates the gap, carriers are generated in the semiconductor and swept by an electric field from the bias applied to the metal electrodes, and the movement of the carriers generates the terahertz wave.  

One of the common semiconductor materials of choice for a \gls{PCA} is \gls{LT} grown indium gallium arsenide (InGaAs), which has a bandgap of $E_{g}<$ \qty{0.8}{\electronvolt} and can be excited by common telecommunications wavelength lasers operating at \qty{1550}{\nano\meter}~\cite{burfordReviewTerahertzPhotoconductive2017}. Another common semiconductor material used for \gls{PCA}s is \gls{LT} gallium arsenide (GaAs), which has a bandgap of $E_{g}<$ \qty{1.42}{\electronvolt} and is operated at a much shorter wavelength of \qty{780}{nm}\cite{delarosaTerahertzEmissionImprovement2024}. However, when a bare PCA is illuminated by a laser there are significant reflection losses due to the high refractive index of the material\cite{riturajHighlySensitiveEfficient2025}. Adding an antireflective coating to the PCA can increase the efficiency of the device by reducing the amount of light reflected by the film~\cite{jiangTerahertzPhotoconductiveAntenna2021}. The challenge lies in the design of a suitable antireflective coating for the aforementioned wavelengths.

\section{Significance of the study}\label{sec: significance}
The development of antireflective coatings which reduce reflection losses at commonly used pump wavelengths for terahertz devices holds great potential for increasing the efficiency of \gls{THz} \gls{PCA}. By enhancing the efficiency of \gls{THz} generation, this study contributes to the advancement of \gls{THz} \gls{TDS} systems, which have diverse applications in fields such as biology, imaging, and pharmaceuticals. The Semiconductors group of the Condensed Matter Physics Laboratory in UP Diliman is able to grow \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-InGaAs layers via \gls{MBE}, fabricate them into \gls{PCA}s via photolithography, and study them using \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} and \qty{780}{\nano\meter} wavelength lasers. However, the \gls{PCA}s being developed lack antireflective coatings optimized for their operating wavelengths. By investigating a mulitlayer \gls{ARC} stack composed of a-Si and SiO$_2$, two materials commonly used for CMOS processing, this study aims to address these problems, improving the \gls{THz} intensity generated by the \gls{PCA} and its overall performance. The findings could pave the way for more efficient \gls{THz} devices, enhancing capabilities in scientific research, medical diagnostics, and industrial processes.

\section{Objectives of the study}\label{sec: Objectives}
The main objective of this study is to investigate the feasibility of using a-Si and SiO$_2$ thin films to create an antireflective coating which can be used for InGaAs and GaAs \gls{PCA}s. Specifically, this will require the following objectives:
\begin{itemize}
	\item to identify the optical properties of a-Si and SiO$_2$ thin films deposited using electron beam evaporation,
	\item to design an antireflective coating utilizing a-Si and SiO$_2$ optimized for the wavelengths at which \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-GaAs THz \gls{PCA}s can be operated, and
	\item to evaluate the reflectance of the fabricated a-Si/SiO$_2$ antireflective coating at the relevant wavelengths.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and limitations of the study}\label{sec: sco}

The study will only cover the investigation of an \gls{a-Si} and SiO$_2$ \gls{ARC} for the wavelengths at which \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-GaAsTHz \gls{PCA} are generally operated. As such, the study will not investigate the growth of \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-GaAs film using \gls{MBE}, nor the integration of the \gls{ARC} with the photoconductive antennas, which will require photolithography and terahertz \gls{TDS}. It will also be limited to non-reactive electron beam evaporation as the deposition method for the \gls{a-Si}/SiO$_2$ and will not inlcude investigation of other parameters during growth such as substrate heating and base pressure. The characterization is also limited to the evaluation of the reflectance of the designed coatings in the range of a Si photodetector (\qtyrange{500}{1000}{\nano\meter}).
