\chapter{Introduction}\label{cha: introduction}
The development of devices which operate in the terahertz range has been a recent area of interest for researchers. The applications of terahertz waves include analysis of biological specimens~\cite{yuMedicalApplicationTerahertz2019}, pharmaceuticals~\cite{dohiApplicationTerahertzPulse2016}, and imaging~\cite{petrovApplicationTerahertzPulse2016} among others. The mechanisms by which these devices operate may include upconversion from the radio and mmWave range, or downconversion of photonic devices from the infrared region~\cite{songReviewIntenseTerahertz2026}. An example of a device which utilizes downconversion is the terahertz \gls{PCA}, and there are already multiple demonstrations and even commercial \gls{PCA} devices that utilize the \qty{780}{\nano\meter} and \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} wavelengths, which are supplied by Ti:sapphire and telecommunication fiber lasers respectively~\cite{yangSimpleReliableOptical2019, PCAPhotoconductiveAntenna}. 

A \gls{PCA} consists of a semiconductor material where the terahertz generation or absorption occurs and two metal electrodes on the semiconductor surface separated by a few microns that forms the photoconductive gap~\cite{songHandbookTerahertzTechnologies2015}. When a femtosecond pulse from the laser illuminates the gap, carriers are generated in the semiconductor and swept by an electric field from the bias applied to the metal electrodes, and the movement of the carriers generates the terahertz wave.  

One of the common semiconductor materials of choice for a \gls{PCA} is \gls{LT} grown indium gallium arsenide (InGaAs), which has a bandgap of $E_{g}<$ \qty{0.8}{\electronvolt} and can be excited by telecommunications wavelength femtosecond fiber lasers operating at \qty{1550}{\nano\meter}~\cite{burfordReviewTerahertzPhotoconductive2017}. Another common semiconductor material used for \gls{PCA}s is \gls{LT} gallium arsenide (GaAs), which has a bandgap of $E_{g}<$ \qty{1.42}{\electronvolt} and operates at a much shorter wavelength of \qty{780}{nm}\cite{delarosaTerahertzEmissionImprovement2024} supplied by femtosecond Ti:sapphire lasers. However, when a bare PCA is illuminated by a laser there are significant reflection losses due to the high refractive index of the material\cite{riturajHighlySensitiveEfficient2025}. Adding an antireflective coating to the PCA can increase the efficiency of the device by reducing the amount of light reflected by the film~\cite{jiangTerahertzPhotoconductiveAntenna2021}. The challenge lies in the design of a suitable antireflective coating for the aforementioned wavelengths.

\section{Significance of the study}\label{sec: significance}
The development of antireflective coatings which reduce reflection losses at commonly used excitation wavelengths for terahertz devices holds potential for increasing the efficiency of \gls{THz} \gls{PCA}. By enhancing the efficiency of \gls{THz} generation \gls{THz} \gls{TDS} systems are improved, which in turn have diverse applications in fields such as biology, imaging, and pharmaceuticals.

The Semiconductors group of the Condensed Matter Physics Laboratory in UP Diliman is able to grow \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-InGaAs layers via \gls{MBE}, fabricate them into \gls{PCA}s via photolithography, and study them using \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} and \qty{780}{\nano\meter} wavelength lasers for \gls{THz} \gls{TDS}. However, the \gls{PCA}s being developed lack antireflective coatings optimized for their operating wavelengths. By developing a mulitlayer antireflective coating stack composed of a-Si and SiO$_2$, two materials commonly used for CMOS processing, this study aims to address these problems, improving the \gls{THz} intensity generated by the \gls{PCA} and its overall performance. The findings could pave the way for more efficient \gls{THz} devices.

\section{Objectives of the study}\label{sec: Objectives}
The main objective of this study is to design, fabricate, and characterize  an antireflective coating based on layers of a-Si and SiO$_2$ thin films  that work at the excitation wavelengths commonly used for \gls{THz} applications. Specifically, this will require the following objectives:
\begin{itemize}
	\item to deposit a-Si and SiO$_2$ thin films using electron beam evaporation,
	\item to determine the material and optical properties of the deposited films,
	\item to design a multilayer antireflective coating of a-Si and SiO$_2$ tailored for the \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} and \qty{780}{\nano\meter} wavelengths using the transfer matrix method,
	\item to fabricate the multilayer antireflective coatings on bare Si wafers, and
	\item to measure the reflectance of the fabricated a-Si/SiO$_2$ antireflective coatings and compare them with the simulated reflectance.
\end{itemize}

\section{Scope and limitations of the study}\label{sec: sco}

This study covers the development of an \gls{a-Si} and SiO$_2$ antireflective coating for the \qty{1550}{\nano\meter} and \qty{780}{\nano\meter} wavelengths which are commonly used for the excitation of \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-GaAsTHz \gls{PCA}. We have not included the integration of the developed antireflective coating with \gls{LT}-InGaAs and \gls{LT}-GaAsTHz \gls{PCA}s, which requires more procedures such as the growth of these materials on GaAs or InP wafers via molecular beam epitaxy, patterning of the antenna via UV photolithography, deposition of metal contacts via electron beam lithography, and investigation of the emission via THz time-domain spectroscopy. We will also limit the variables for the deposition method of electron beam evaporation to the thickness of the \gls{a-Si} and SiO$_2$ films and will not inlcude investigation of other growth parameters such as substrate heating and base pressure. The substrates used in this study are limited to polished Si wafers and glass, which are readily available and much cheaper compared to epi-ready semi-insulating GaAs. Our reflection measurements are also limited by the wavelength range of the existing Si photodetector (\qtyrange{500}{1000}{\nano\meter}).
